A field guide for the gigging guitarist

OPEN tunings

Seven tunings, the chord grips that unlock each one, why each sounds the way it does, and songs you can play tonight. Stage moves first: the fastest peg routes from standard, ranked by how few strings you have to touch between songs.

⚡ Stage Moves Open G Open D DADGAD Open E Open A Open C Open Dm

Stage moves: getting there from standard

Every card shows the six pegs, low string on the left. Gold means tune down, coral means tune up, greyed pegs stay put. Ranked from quickest onstage move to "do this at the set break."

01

DADGAD

D A D G A D3 pegs · all down a whole step

The move: strings 6, 2, and 1 each drop exactly one whole step. Same interval three times, so your ear locks in fast: drop the low E to D, then match strings 2 and 1 to it by octaves.

02

Open G

D G D G B D3 pegs · all down a whole step

The move: strings 6, 5, and 1 down a whole step. The middle four strings of standard never move, so partial chord shapes you already know survive. Keith Richards removes the 6th string entirely and treats string 5 as the root.

03

Open D

D A D F# A D4 pegs · all down

The move: three whole steps and one half step (G drops just a fret's worth to F#). Fastest route: get to DADGAD first with the three-peg move above, then nudge the 3rd string down one more half step. Open D is the hub tuning: one peg away from DADGAD, one peg from Open Dm, and a capo away from Open E.

04

Open D minor

D A D F A D4 pegs · all down

The move: identical to Open D except the 3rd string falls a full whole step, G to F. If you are already in Open D, this is a single half-step nudge on one peg.

05

Open E

E B E G# B E3 pegs · UP · extra tension

The move: strings 5 and 4 up a whole step, string 3 up a half step. Tuning up adds real tension: fine on a modern electric with 9s or 10s, risky on a vintage flat-top. The pro dodge: tune Open D and capo at fret 2. Identical pitches, zero added stress, and you keep the D-family shapes.

06

Open A

E A E A C# E3 pegs · UP · extra tension

The move: strings 4, 3, 2 climb (two whole steps and a half). Same tension caveat as Open E, and the same dodge: Open G with a capo at fret 2 is Open A for all practical purposes. Everything you learn on the Open G page works here, up a whole step.

07

Open C

C G C G C E5 pegs · big drops · set-break tuning

The move: the low E falls two whole steps to C, strings 5 and 4 down a whole step, and string 2 actually rises a half step to C. Too much drama for a between-song switch. Do it at the break, or bring a second guitar living in C. Heavier strings on the bottom (a 56 or a light baritone set) keep the low C from flapping.

One-peg pivots between open tunings

Open D → DADGAD

3rd string up a half step, F# to G. One peg, two worlds.

Open D → Open Dm

3rd string down a half step, F# to F. Major turns minor with one twist.

DADGAD → Open Dm

3rd string down a whole step, G to F. Still one peg.

Open C → Open C6

5th string up a whole step, G to A. C6 (C A C G C E) is the actual Zeppelin tuning for Friends and Bron-Yr-Aur.

Open D + capo 2 = Open E

Same pitches, no extra string tension. This is how most slide players "play in Open E."

Open G + capo 2 = Open A

The classic Delta blues dodge. Robert Johnson keys move with the capo.

Setlist and hardware tips

  • Order the set by tuning family. D family runs Open D → DADGAD → Open Dm on single-peg pivots. G family covers Open G and, via capo, Open A. Cross between families only once or twice a night.
  • Tune down between songs, never up. Strings settle into lower tension almost immediately; tuned-up strings drift flat for a couple of minutes. Another argument for the capo dodges on E and A.
  • Detune, overshoot, come back up. When you do have to raise a string, drop it below target and tune up to pitch. The string winds seat under tension and hold better.
  • Clip-on tuner lives on the headstock all night. Mute with your palm, turn pegs with your fret hand, and you can make the three-peg DADGAD move in under 20 seconds while you talk to the room.
  • Stretch new strings hard before an open-tuning gig. Retuning cycles expose any string that has not fully settled.
  • Two-guitar luxury: keep one guitar in standard/D-family, one in G-family or Open C. Heavier gauge (11s or 12s) on the low-tuning guitar.

Open G

D G D G B D

Why it works. Strum everything open and you get a fat G major with the 5th on the bottom. Because the middle four strings are untouched from standard, this is the friendliest open tuning to visit: the root sits on string 5, and one straight barre finger gives you a full major chord anywhere on the neck. That one-finger-per-chord economy is why it became the engine of both Delta blues slide (the bar replaces the finger) and the Rolling Stones' entire second act: Keith's style is the open barre plus two fingers hammering the sus4 and 6th on top, that snapping "Start Me Up" flick.

Great for: slide blues, Stones-style riff rock, country blues fingerpicking, banjo-flavored folk (it is literally banjo tuning, strings 5 to 1).

Open D

D A D F# A D

Why it works. Root on the bottom this time, so the low string is the tonic and the whole guitar rings like one giant D chord with three octaves of D in it. That bottom-heavy voicing is why it owns slide blues (Elmore James) and gospel slide (Blind Willie Johnson), and why folk players from Richie Havens to Joni Mitchell built on it: thumb the low D, and melody and chords float on top of an endless drone. The barre logic is the same one-finger-anywhere deal as Open G, just with the root under your finger instead of the 5th.

Great for: slide in D, gospel blues, open-string folk strumming (Havens strummed almost everything with a thumb barre), and as the hub of the whole D family of tunings.

DADGAD

D A D G A D

Why it works. Strictly speaking not an "open" tuning: strummed open it gives Dsus4, a chord that is neither major nor minor. That ambiguity is the entire point. With no 3rd committing you to a mode, DADGAD floats between D major, D minor, D Mixolydian, and D Dorian, which is exactly the modal territory of Irish, Scottish, and Middle Eastern music. Davey Graham devised it in the early 60s after hearing oud players in Morocco; Bert Jansch and Jimmy Page ran with it. Melodies sit on the top two strings (tuned a 4th apart, easy fingering) while the bottom drones like a set of pipes.

Great for: Celtic fingerstyle, drop-thumb drone folk, modern fingerstyle percussion players, and the mighty Zeppelin drone-riff.

Open E

E B E G# B E

Why it works. Open D's voicing shifted up a whole step, with the extra string tension giving a brighter, harder snap that electric slide players love: this is Duane Allman's home tuning, and the jangly sparkle under Joni Mitchell's early records. The low E root means you keep the same bottom string as standard, so riffs that pedal off the low string feel natural. Interior sus4 and 6th embellishments fall under one hammering finger, which is the entire harmonic engine of She Talks to Angels.

Great for: electric slide (Allman, Derek Trucks), open-chord rock balladry, Delta-to-Chicago blues in E. On acoustics or vintage guitars, reach it as Open D capo 2.

Open A

E A E A C# E

Why it works. Open G's twin, one whole step up: the 5th sits on the bottom, root on string 5, and every Open G grip transfers directly. The higher tension gives it bark, and having low E and A both available makes it a natural for droning blues in A, the guitarist's favorite blues key. This is prime Robert Johnson territory (his A-position songs) and where Zeppelin went for their heaviest slide moments. Most players in practice reach it as Open G capo 2, but electrics with light strings wear the real tuning fine.

Great for: Delta and hill-country blues in A, heavy slide, one-chord trance boogie.

Open C

C G C G C E

Why it works. The deep one. Dropping the 6th string two whole steps to C puts you in near-baritone territory, and with three Cs and two Gs ringing, the guitar turns into a small orchestra: huge fundamentals below, a lone major 3rd on top carrying all the color. Fingerstyle players love it because bass, harmony, and melody separate cleanly into registers; percussive modern players love how much it moves air. John Fahey built American Primitive guitar partly on C tunings, and one peg turn (5th string G up to A) converts it to the C6 tuning Jimmy Page used for Friends and Bron-Yr-Aur.

Great for: solo fingerstyle, percussive acoustic (John Butler, Ben Howard territory), low-and-huge accompaniment, writing when you are tired of every chord you know.

Open D minor

D A D F A D

Why it works. The old players called it cross-note tuning. Take Open D and let the 3rd fall from F# to F, and the whole guitar turns mournful: strummed open it is a pure D minor chord, and because the minor 3rd sits on a fretted-string away from major, one finger at the first fret of string 3 flips the entire instrument from minor to major. That flicker between the two, neither fully settled, is the sound of Skip James and the eeriest corner of the Delta. It also barres like the others: one finger gives you a minor chord anywhere.

Great for: haunted Delta blues, minor-key fingerstyle, drones with a shadow over them. The cheapest ticket to sounding like nobody else at the open mic.
